Four Things Had to Show Up at Once: What 75 Years of AI History Teaches Leaders

Built for: healthcare and public-sector leaders deciding whether an AI project is ready.

Introduction

Most AI timelines read like a highlight reel of geniuses. That's fun to watch, but it doesn't help a hospital administrator or an agency director decide whether the AI tool on their desk is ready to use.

This artifact retells the history of AI through one simple idea: AI only worked when four things showed up at the same time. Good ideas, enough computing power, enough data, and steady money. When one was missing, progress stalled, sometimes for a decade. When all four lined up in 2012, everything changed. The good news for leaders is that the same four ingredients make a quick, practical test for any AI project today.

Description

The artifact has three parts you can use right on this page:

1. An interactive four-lane timeline (1950 to 2025). Every milestone sits in the lane of the ingredient it added. Gray bands mark the two AI winters, hollow dots mark setbacks, and the dashed line marks 2012, the first year all four lanes lined up. Click any dot to read what happened.

2. The Four-Ingredient Check. Four plain questions that turn the history into a test for your own AI project, with a lesson from history behind each one.

3. The full 13-slide timeline deck in PowerPoint and PDF, with era-by-era detail, the causes and impacts of both AI winters, and a fact-check of a popular AI history video.

Objective

To give non-technical leaders a fast, honest way to judge AI projects by learning from the field's own history, and to show how I research, fact-check, and explain technical topics in plain language.

Process

  1. Read the sources. I started with a popular AI history video, an IEEE Computer Society article, and Our World in Data's history of AI, then checked key claims against original papers and records.
  2. Fact-checked before building. I found five claims in the video that didn't hold up. For example, it credits Geoffrey Hinton with inventing convolutional networks in 2006, but Yann LeCun was already using them in 1989.
  3. Sorted the milestones. I placed about 40 events into four lanes, one per ingredient, and marked both AI winters with their causes and impacts.
  4. Found the pattern. Both winters began when ideas ran ahead of the chips and data, and the money left. 2012 was the first year all four lanes lined up.
  5. Built it twice. I designed the full deck in PowerPoint, then rebuilt the timeline as an interactive web version for this site.
  6. Tailored it for leaders. I cut technical terms and added the Four-Ingredient Check, so the history becomes a tool someone can use in their next budget meeting.
  7. Revised the concept. My first draft compared lab dates with everyday-use dates. When I reread the assignment, I saw it didn't explain cloud computing or the causes of the winters well enough, so I rebuilt it around the four ingredients.

Value Proposition

Unique Value

Instead of another list of dates, this artifact explains why AI stalled and why it took off, then turns that pattern into a test leaders can actually use. It also shows a habit I bring to every project: I checked the sources first and corrected five errors in a widely shared video before building anything on top of it.

Relevance

Hospitals and government agencies are pitched AI tools constantly, often with demos that look finished. The Four-Ingredient Check gives them four plain questions to ask before they commit money. That connects directly to my goal of helping healthcare and public-sector teams trust the data and decisions behind AI.
